    Section 1.4 Derivatives

    This section will be a condensed version of about 150 pages of a calculus textbook. That is, this will be an extremely-brief review of derivatives.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.1 The Derivative

    The average velocity of an object over a time interval \(\Delta t\) is defined as its change in its position \(\Delta s\) divided by \(\Delta t\text{.}\) That is, \(\displaystyle v_{avg} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\text{.}\) If a function \(f(x)\) models movement of an object, then the average rate of change of that object over a time interval \(\Delta x\) is given by \(\displaystyle ARC = \frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\text{,}\) where \(\Delta f\) is the total change in the function. So, if the time interval is from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\text{,}\) then the average rate of change in \(f(x)\) is given by \(\displaystyle ARC_{[a,b]} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b-a}\text{.}\) Graphically, if we draw a line through the points \((a,f(a))\) and \((b,f(b))\text{,}\) then its slope, \(\displaystyle \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\) is the average rate of change of the function from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\text{.}\) We call such a line a secant line.
    🔗
    Average rates of change are fine, but a more interesting concept is the {instantaneous velocity of an object - the velocity of an object at a singular point in time. We can approximate instantaneous velocity by taking very small time intervals. Let \(a\)> be the starting time and let $h$ be some time interval (so ending time is \(a+h\)). Then we can change our average rate of change function to \({\displaystyle ARC_{[a,a+h]} = \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}}\text{.}\) When \(h\approx 0\text{,}\) then the average rate of change is approximately the instantaneous rate of change. Of course, we can’t have \(h=0\text{,}\) but we can make \(h\) as small as we would like - the smaller the better. (You smell that? That’s the impending arrival of limits.)
    🔗
    We can find the instantaneous velocity of a object by taking the limit as \(h\) approaches \(0\) of our average rate of change function, assuming that limit exists. This limit is called the derivative of \(>f(x)\) at \(x=a\text{.}\) It is denoted by \(f'(a)\).
    🔗

    Definition 1.4.2.

    The derivative of \(f(x)\) at \(x=a\) is
    \begin{equation*} f'(a) = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}, \end{equation*}
    provided that the limit exists.
    🔗
    🔗
    Graphically, the derivative is the slope of the line that locally intersects the function $f(x)$ at only the point \((a,f(a))\text{.}\) That is, it is the limit of the secant lines as $b$ approaches \(a\text{.}\) We call this line the tangent line. This is visualized below.
    🔗
    Suppose we would like to find the instantaneous rate of change of a function at multiple points. It would be handy to have a function that could give us these values rather than calculating the derivative at several points. Fortunately, such a function exists and is called the derivative function.
    🔗

    Definition 1.4.4.

    The derivative function of \(f(x)\) is
    \begin{equation*} {f'(x) = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}}, \end{equation*}
    provided that the limit exits.
    🔗
    🔗
    Just for the sake of completion, we will find the derivative function of \(f(x) = x^3 - 4x\) using the limit definition.
    🔗

    Example 1.4.5. Derivative of \(f(x) = x^3 - 4x\).

    Find the derivative function of \({f(x) = x^3 - 4x}\text{,}\) using the limit definition of a derivative.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{(x+h)^3 - 4(x+h) - (x^3 - 4x)}{h}\\ &= \lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{x^3 + 3x^2 h + 3xh^2 + h^3 - 4x - 4h - x^3 + 4x}{h}\\ &= \lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{3x^2 h + 3xh^2 + h^3 - 4h}{h}\\ &= \lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{h(3x^2 - 4 + 3xh + h^2)}{h}\\ &= \lim_{h\rightarrow 0} 3x^2 - 4 + 3xh + h^2\\ &= 3x^2 - 4 + 0 + 0\\ &= 3x^2 - 4\\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    There. We did an example. Now let’s never use the limit definition to calculate a derivative again. The important thing to take away from this section is that the derivative represents the rate of change of a function at a specified point.
    🔗

    Example 1.4.6. Derivative of \(|x|\).

    Explain why the derivative of \(f(x) = |x|\) does not exist at \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Consider secant lines for \(|x|\) to the left and right of \(x=0\text{.}\) Take a secant line from \(x=a\) to \(x=0\) for \(a< 0\text{.}\) The slope of this secant line is \({\frac{f(0)-f(a)}{0-a} = \frac{0 - |a|}{-a} = \frac{-(-a)}{-a} = -1}\text{.}\) (Note that \(|a| = -a\) since \(a< 0\text{.}\)) So, all secant lines to the left of \(x = 0\) will have slope \(-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Take a secant line from \(x=0\) to \(x=b\) with \(b>0\text{.}\) The slope of this secant line is \({\frac{f(b)-f(0)}{b-0} = \frac{|b| - 0}{b} = \frac{b}{b} = 1}\text{.}\) So, all secant lines to the right of \(x = 0\) will have slope \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now consider the full limit of the slope of secant lines at \(x=0\text{.}\) To the left, the limit is simply \(-1\text{.}\) To the right, the limit is \(1\text{.}\) Since the left and right limits are not equal, the limit does not exist. So, the derivative cannot exist at \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.2 Derivative Shortcuts

    Fortunately, there are algebraic shortcuts for finding the derivative function of any elementary function. We give a list of those rules below. First, recall the notation \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} f(x)\text{.}\) This is an operator meaning "the derivative of \(f(x)\text{.}\)""
    🔗

    Derivative Shortcut Rules.

    • For any constant \(c\text{,}\) \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} c = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • For any constant \(c\text{,}\) \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} cx = c\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • For any real number \(n\text{,}\) \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} x^n = nx^{n-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • For any \(a>0\text{,}\) \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} a^x = a^x \ln(a)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} e^x = e^x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} \log_a(x) = \frac{1}{\ln(a) x}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} \ln(x) = \frac{1}{x}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} \sin(x) = \cos(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} \cos(x) = -\sin(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} \arctan(x) = \frac{1}{1+x^2}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Constant Rule: \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} c\cdot f(x) = c\cdot f'(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Sum Rule: \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} [f(x) \pm g(x)] = f'(x) \pm g'(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Chain Rule: \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Product Rule: \(\displaystyle {\frac{d}{dx} \left(f(x)\cdot g(x)\right) = f(x)\cdot g'(x) + f'(x) \cdot g(x)}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Quotient Rule: \(\displaystyle {\frac{d}{dx} \left(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right) = \frac{g(x)\cdot f'(x) - f(x)\cdot g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • "Mini Chain Rule": \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} f(ax+b) = a\cdot f'(ax+b)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Pay attention to the last rule, that I like to call the "mini chain rule." Many students pick this up after doing a number of derivative examples, but I find it helpful to see it explicitly. If you have a function that is a composition of functions, but the inside function is linear, then the derivative is simply the derivative of the outside function, with the inside function plugged in, times the slope of the linear function. For example, the derivative of \({\sin(-2x+7)}\) is \(\cos(-2x+7) \cdot (-2) = -2\cos(-2x+7)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.4.7.

    Find the derivative functions of the following functions.
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle 5x^{4.3} - e^{7x} + 3\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle (9-x^2)^8 + \frac{5}{x^3}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle 9\arctan({5x^2})\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle \ln(7x)\cos(x)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle 5^{x}-\frac{8x^2-1}{\ln(5x-2)}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Applying the chain rule to \(\displaystyle e^{7x}\text{,}\) we get \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} (5x^{4.3} - e^{7x} + 3) = 5\cdot 4.3 x^{3.3} - e^{7x}\cdot \left(\frac{d}{dx} 7x\right) + 0 = 21.5x^{3.3} - 7e^{7x}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Applying the chain rule to \((9-x^2)^8\) and noticing that \(\frac{5}{x^3} = 5x^{-3}\text{,}\) we get \({\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} \left( (9-x^2)^8 + \frac{5}{x^3}\right) = 8(9-x^2)^7 \cdot \left(\frac{d}{dx} 9-x^2\right) + 5\cdot (-3)x^{-4} = -16x(9-x^2)^7 - \frac{15}{x^4}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Applying the chain rule to \(\arctan(5x^2)\text{,}\) we get \({\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} \left(9\arctan(5x^2)\right) = 9\cdot \frac{1}{1+(5x^2)^2}\cdot \left(\frac{d}{dx} 5x^2\right) = \frac{9}{1+25x^4}\cdot 10x = \frac{90x}{1+25x^4}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Applying the chain rule to \(\ln(7x)\) yields its derivative as \({\displaystyle \frac{7}{7x} = \frac{1}{x}}\text{.}\) Then, we use the product rule to get \({\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} \left(\ln(7x)\cos(x)\right) = \ln(7x) \cdot \left(\frac{d}{dx} \cos(x)\right) + \left(\frac{d}{dx} \ln(7x)\right)\cdot \cos(x)} {= -\ln(7x)\sin(x) + \frac{\cos(x)}{x}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. We will need to use the chain rule for \(\ln(5x-2)\) to get its derivative of \(\displaystyle \frac{5}{5x-2}\text{.}\) Then applying the quotient rule yields \({\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx} \left(5^x - \frac{8x^2 - 1}{\ln(5x-2)}\right) = \ln(5) \cdot 5^x - \frac{\ln(5x-2)\cdot \left(\frac{d}{dx} 8x^2 - 1\right) - (8x^2-1)\cdot \left(\frac{d}{dx} \ln(5x-2)\right)}{[\ln(5x-2)]^2}} {= \ln(5)\cdot 5^x - \frac{\ln(5x-2)\cdot (16x) - (8x^2 - 1)\cdot \frac{5}{5x-2}}{[\ln(5x-2)]^2}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Fortunately, you will not need to calculate derivatives that are as complicated as the preceding ones on the P exam.

    Checkpoint 1.4.8. Find the Derivative 1.

    Find the derivative of \(4x^{2} e^{-x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(f'(x)=\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\exp\mathopen{}\left(-x\right)\mathopen{}\left(8x^{1}-4x^{2}\right)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the product rule and noting from the mini-chain rule that \(\frac{d}{dx} e^{-x} = -e^{-x}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx} \left(4x^{2} e^{-x}\right) \amp = \left(\frac{d}{dx} 4x^{2}\right)\cdot e^{-x} + 4x^{2}\cdot \left(\frac{d}{dx} e^{-x}\right)\\ \amp = {8}x^{{1}} e^{-x} + 4x^{2}\left(-e^{-x}\right)\\ \amp =e^{-x}\left({8}x^{{1}} - 4x^{2}\right). \end{aligned} \end{equation*}

    Checkpoint 1.4.20. Find the Derivative 13.

    Let \(g(x)=\frac{1}{s\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2s^2}}\text{,}\) where \(s>0\text{.}\) Find the range of \(x\) values for which \(g'(x)\) is positive.
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle x<m\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle x>m\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle x=m\)
      🔗

      🔗

    🔗

    \(\text{A}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    The constant factor \(\frac{1}{s\sqrt{2\pi}}\) is positive. Differentiating the exponential part gives
    🔗
